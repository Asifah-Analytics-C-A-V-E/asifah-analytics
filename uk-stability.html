🧭 Situation — as of November 4, 2026

Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July 2026 after replacing Keir Starmer as Labour leader — Britain's seventh prime minister in ten years. John Healey moved from Defence to the Treasury and delivered his first Budget on 28 October; markets took it calmly and the fiscal rules held. Labour and Reform UK have swapped the polling lead since the summer. In Scotland, the May 2026 election returned a pro-independence majority at Holyrood (SNP 58, Greens 15 of 129).

The So What: Britain is not a conflict state; it is a pressure state, and the pressure runs through money. The City of London is where much of the world's influence is banked, insured and priced — which makes it both Britain's quiet global reach and its soft underbelly. Three things decide whether the UK's story stays political or becomes a stability story: whether the gilt market keeps trusting the Treasury, whether money and people keep choosing London, and whether the overseas edge (Falklands, Chagos, Akrotiri) stays a diplomatic file rather than a military one.

🌍 The overseas edge — territories & bases
Where a British domestic decision becomes someone else's security question. Dated facts; re-verified on a 45-day cycle (next ~Dec 19, 2026).

🇫🇰 Falklands & the South Atlantic

Argentina re-opened the dispute in September 2026 after President Trump declined to say the US would back Britain; Milei sanctioned firms on the Sea Lion oil project. Britain's garrison and Typhoons at Mount Pleasant outclass anything Argentina can field. The islands and South Georgia anchor Britain's Antarctic claim.
Watch: US wording on sovereignty · Sea Lion contractors · incidents at sea · see the Argentina page

🇬🇮 Gibraltar

The UK–EU treaty on Gibraltar was signed in July 2026: the border fence with Spain came down and checks moved to the port and airport, with Schengen-style controls run with EU officials. The naval base and its submarine berth stay British.
Watch: ratification and implementation glitches · Spanish politics · any change to military access

🏝️ Chagos & Diego Garcia

Britain signed a treaty in May 2025 to hand the Chagos Islands to Mauritius while keeping the US–UK base on Diego Garcia on a long lease. Ratification stalled in 2026 after Washington turned against the deal; Burnham told Mauritius in September he would work "with the USA" towards ratification.
Watch: the Commons bill · US statements · Chinese interest in Mauritius · base access during Middle East operations

🇨🇾 Akrotiri & Dhekelia (Cyprus)

Britain's two Sovereign Base Areas. On 2 March 2026 an Iranian-made drone launched from Lebanon hit RAF Akrotiri, causing limited damage — the first strike on a British base in the Iran war. Akrotiri flies strike and surveillance missions over the Middle East.
Watch: drone or rocket incidents · Cypriot politics on the bases · see the Cyprus page
So What: each of these is a promise Britain made to someone else — islanders, Washington, Mauritius, Cyprus. The common thread in 2026 is the American guarantee: on the Falklands and on Chagos, Washington's position moved and London had to adjust. Read the territories as a gauge of how much room Britain has to act on its own.

🏦How the City works — and why others use it
▼

The machinery: English law and courts that foreigners trust, a deep property market, the London Stock Exchange, Lloyd's of London, the London Metal Exchange, the gold market and Bank of England vaults, and the network of overseas territories and Crown Dependencies with their own company registries. Together they make London the place where a lot of the world's money is held, insured and argued over.

Why it matters for influence: after 2022 the "Londongrad" years ended in sanctions (OFSI freezes, the register of overseas entities, unexplained wealth orders) — but enforcement has been slow. Gulf sovereign funds are now among the largest buyers of British assets, from property to football clubs, and in 2024–25 the fight over who may own a national newspaper (the Telegraph) became a test case. Chinese money is smaller but politically sharper (the Royal Mint Court embassy).

📉September 2022 — when the gilt market ended a government
▼

On 23 September 2022 the "mini-budget" announced large unfunded tax cuts. Within days the pound hit a record low against the dollar and long-dated gilt yields jumped; pension funds using leveraged "LDI" strategies faced collateral calls, and on 28 September the Bank of England began emergency bond buying. Liz Truss resigned on 20 October. The FTSE 100, full of overseas earners, barely moved — the damage was in the pound, gilts and the government's credibility.

Why it is the template: it showed that a bond market can discipline a British government in weeks. The credibility check on this page looks for that shape: yields up and the pound down at once.

🏛️Politics — Burnham, Reform and the union
▼

Government: Andy Burnham (PM since 20 July 2026), Chancellor John Healey, Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband. Labour won a large majority in July 2024; the next general election is due by mid-2029.

Opposition: Reform UK under Nigel Farage led the polls for much of 2025–26; Labour drew level after Burnham took over. The Conservatives (Kemi Badenoch) trail both.

The union: the May 2026 Holyrood election left the SNP the largest party (58 of 129) and a pro-independence majority with the Greens; John Swinney is pressing for a second referendum. In Northern Ireland the Windsor Framework governs trade across the Irish Sea.

🛡️Defence, AUKUS & Ukraine
▼

Nuclear: Britain's deterrent is four Vanguard-class submarines with Trident missiles, based at Faslane in Scotland — which is why Scottish independence is also a defence question.

AUKUS: with the US and Australia, Britain is designing the next generation of nuclear-powered attack submarines (SSN-AUKUS); Australia's boats will be part-built in Adelaide and part in Barrow-in-Furness.

Ukraine: Britain has been one of Kyiv's largest military backers (Storm Shadow missiles, training) and co-leads the "coalition of the willing" planning for a post-ceasefire force. Russian spy ships and submarines around British cables and pipelines are a standing concern.
