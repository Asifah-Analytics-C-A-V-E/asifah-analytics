🧭 Situation — as of November 4, 2026

Mark Carney (Liberal) has been prime minister since March 2025. Canada is in an open trade war with its closest ally: after talks broke down in August 2026, US tariffs on a broad range of Canadian goods took effect on August 22 — the first to hit USMCA-compliant goods — and Washington announced a 50% tariff on cars, trucks, parts and steel from January 1, 2027. Canada answered with counter-tariffs on C$27.6bn of US goods from September 8. At the July USMCA review the US declined to renew the agreement as it stands.

Unity files: Alberta voted about 75% to stay in Canada in a non-binding referendum on October 19, 2026 (researchers flagged Russian- and US-linked online accounts amplifying separation calls). In Quebec, the sovereigntist Parti Québécois won a minority government on October 5 (59 of 127 seats) and has promised a sovereignty referendum — possibly after 2029.

The So What: Canada's stability risk is economic and constitutional, not violent. About three-quarters of its exports go to the US, so the trade war hits Ontario's auto belt and Alberta's oil directly — and economic pain is what feeds both unity files. The country has rallied around the flag for now; the test is whether that holds through the January auto tariff.

🧊 Arctic Gateway & Critical Minerals

The gateway: the Northwest Passage runs through Canada's Arctic islands. Ottawa says it is Canadian internal waters; Washington and others say it is an international strait. As the ice retreats it becomes a shipping route between the Atlantic and Pacific, and whoever controls access controls the route.

Who is pressing: the ally and the adversaries at once. The US disputes the passage and is pressing on Greenland next door. Russia dominates the Arctic militarily, and Chinese research and coast-guard vessels have operated in the region, including joint Russia-China patrols near Alaska. Canada answered with expanded Arctic military operations in 2026, NORAD modernisation, and new polar icebreakers built with the US and Finland.

Critical minerals: the North holds nickel, rare earths and other critical minerals that both Washington and Beijing want; Canada wants them processed at home and sold to allies.

So What: Canada's Arctic is contested from both sides. A US that treats Canada as a pressure target and a Russia-China pairing that treats the Arctic as open water are two different threats to the same sovereignty. This pairs with the Greenland page — the two halves of the North American Arctic gateway.
Watch
  • Northwest Passage transits — and whether a foreign government sends a ship through without asking
  • Chinese or Russian vessels or aircraft near Canada's Arctic and Alaska
  • NORAD modernisation and icebreaker milestones
  • US moves on Greenland — the same gateway, one island east

🚚The Trade War, step by step
▼

2025: US "fentanyl" and steel/aluminium tariffs; Canada retaliates; the US Supreme Court later strikes down the IEEPA tariffs (February 2026).

2026: the USMCA review (July) ends without renewal; talks collapse in August; broad US tariffs from August 22 — the first to hit USMCA goods; Canadian counter-tariffs on C$27.6bn from September 8; US bans on some Canadian imports (alcohol, dairy, motorcycles) in September; a 50% auto/steel tariff announced for January 1, 2027.

🍁Alberta, Quebec and National Unity
▼

Alberta: the October 19, 2026 referendum asked whether to begin a binding process toward independence; about three-quarters said no. Premier Danielle Smith says she will not hold another vote while in office, but separatist groups say they will continue, and the autonomy agenda (pipelines, provincial powers) remains.

Quebec: Paul St-Pierre Plamondon's Parti Québécois governs with a minority from October 2026 and has promised a third sovereignty referendum (after 1980 and 1995, when "No" won by 50.6%).

🧊Arctic Sovereignty
▼

Canada's claim rests on the Arctic archipelago as historic internal waters. The 1988 Arctic Cooperation Agreement with the US sidestepped the dispute ("agree to disagree"). NORAD — the joint US-Canada air-defence command — is being modernised for cruise-missile and Arctic threats.
